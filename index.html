Before anything: your sanctuary setup

The essentials the platform checks: quiet, bright, professional, reliable.

Environment

  • Audio: free from background noise: pets, household activity, traffic, appliance or notification sounds.
  • Video: sufficient, clear lighting; a plain or neutral background free from clutter. Virtual backgrounds only on the student's explicit request or to briefly manage interruptions, and keep them conservative.
  • Attire: presentable and professional. No sleepwear; sleeveless shirts are prohibited. A plain cardigan or jacket is always a safe layer.

Equipment

  • Computer only; tablets are strictly prohibited. Windows 11 / macOS 10.14+, dual-core CPU, 8 GB RAM.
  • Antivirus active and updated; genuine OS, firewall on, lock your computer when away.
  • Internet: minimum 3 Mbps up/down, recommended 15 Mbps. Wired LAN preferred. Close other apps and pause downloads during lessons.
  • Browser: latest Google Chrome (Firefox as backup).
  • Webcam + dedicated headset with a noise-cancelling microphone (DSP echo reduction and in-line controls are ideal).
Lesson Room: lessons happen in RareJob's in-house platform (text, voice, and video) directly on the website, with no third-party call apps.

The rhythm of a 25-minute lesson

A gentle timeline you can follow every single time.

  • ~5 min before
    Open the booked slot's Tutoring Notes page. Review the lesson info, student info, student preferences (requested material, talk time, pacing, error-correction style), and recent notes from previous tutors. Refresh the page around 5 minutes before start for last-minute updates.
  • 5–1 min before
    Click Enter Lesson Room. Run your environment check (mic, camera, connection) and wait warmly for your student.
  • Minute 0–2
    Greet the student and give a brief, formal self-introduction. Confirm the material and their learning goals; ask about any specific requests. Share the material link via chat box if needed.
  • The lesson
    Follow the Tutor Guide on the right side of the material. Correct errors according to the student's stated preferences, and use the chat box as your digital blackboard for spellings, corrections, and supplementary notes.
  • 5–3 min before end
    Begin wrapping up: give feedback as specified in the material's Tutor Guide, then use the remaining time for final questions or anything the student wants to revisit.
  • Minute 25
    Thank the student verbally and formally announce the conclusion before ending the call. Never end even a few seconds early (undertime complaints) and don't extend past 25 minutes. Respect the schedule so back-to-back lessons start on time.
Consecutive lessons with the same student: both of you exit the finished Lesson Room, close that window, then enter the next slot's room to avoid technical issues.

When the student is late, absent, or cancels

The rules with real deadlines, worth memorizing.

  • File a "Student is Late" trouble report if the student is still unavailable around 2–3 minutes after start time; the option only exists within the first 10 minutes. Also file it if the student disconnects or the lesson can't start promptly due to issues on their end. Failing to file it can affect your evaluation.
  • Whatever happens, stay inside the Lesson Room until the end of the reserved time, because the student may still decide to continue.
  • If the student files a Lesson Trouble Report after cancelling, respond with the Over 10 Minutes option.
  • Absent status in the notes can only be selected from the 25th minute of the scheduled lesson.

Teaching Japanese learners with a soft touch

Small kindnesses that make a big difference for your students.

  • Honor the pause. Silence usually means thinking, not disengagement. Count to five before rescuing, since many Japanese learners need a beat to build the sentence.
  • Gentle pronunciation care: common katakana-English carryovers include l/r, th, v/b, and adding vowels to word endings ("cake-u"). Model the sound warmly, have them echo, and praise the attempt.
  • Ask, don't quiz. Open questions ("What do you think about…?") invite more talk than yes/no questions.
  • Praise specifics. "Your past tense was perfect in that story" lands far better than "good job."
  • Recycle their world. Weave in what you learned from their previous notes (hobbies, work, travel plans) so lessons feel personal (log these in My Students 🐾).
  • Correct by preference. Some students want every error caught; others want flow. Their Lesson Request Details tell you which. Follow it.

How a Jitsuyo Eikaiwa lesson flows

The JE curriculum follows the same spine every lesson. Timings are approximate; protect the Challenge and Feedback at the end, since your notes depend on them.

Introduce · ~2 min
Greet & set the goal
Warm greeting, then read the lesson goal together ("Today, let's talk about old friends"). Ask the hook question from the material, then move on briskly.
Present · ~5 min
Step A Expressions + Step B Pronunciation
Go over each target expression; have the student read the example dialogues aloud. Then drill the pronunciation point (e.g., the dropped /t/ in "can't believe"). Model it, they echo.
Understand · ~6 min
Reading (+ trivia if time)
Student reads the passage aloud. Check comprehension with the material's questions and confirm they caught the target expressions in context. Trivia is a bonus, not a must.
Practice · ~5 min
Step A & Step B exercises
Step A: choose the best sentence. Step B: complete the posts with the expressions. Correct gently right here; practice is where mistakes are cheapest.
Challenge · ~4 min
The real task (your feedback goldmine)
Challenge 1 is the graded task (e.g., reply to an old friend's email using today's expressions). Let them speak; note their exact words for You said / Better. Challenge 2 only if time truly allows.
Feedback · ~3 min
Score + personalized feedback
Announce the Lesson Goal Achievement score kindly, then walk through Range, Accuracy, Fluency, and Interaction with their own sentences. Build the note below while it's fresh.
During Challenge 1, jot the raw quotes. The JE note lives or dies on real "You said" lines. Keep a scratchpad (or the My Memo box) open and capture 3 or 4 exact sentences while they speak; polishing them comes after.

BCC mock lesson guide

Business students expect polish. Warmth stays in your eyes; the delivery stays crisp.

The BCC lesson shape

  • Open professionally (min 0–1): "Hello! Thank you for joining today's lesson. Shall we begin?" Brief, confident, no long chit-chat unless the student initiates.
  • Follow the Jissen material faithfully: its Tutor Guide and flow differ from regular materials, so read the whole lesson beforehand, including the scoring rubric at the end.
  • Correct like a coach: note errors silently in your notepad as the student speaks; deliver corrections at natural pauses via the chat box. Never interrupt an executive mid-thought.
  • Guard the clock: business lessons are graded on time management. Reserve the final 3 minutes for scores and feedback, no matter what.
  • Feedback ritual: paste the accomplished score note into the chat first, then walk through it verbally: strengths, then one or two sharp improvement points with examples.

Professional demeanor

  • Smart casual attire, tidy background, camera on and at eye level.
  • "Smile with your eyes"; attentive nods over big reactions.
  • Precise praise ("Your phrasing of the proposal was very natural") instead of "very good" on repeat.
  • Neutral topics only; redirect gracefully if conversation drifts.

Assessment & scoring reminders

  • The scoring system for each BCC lesson is printed at the end of the material itself; read it before the lesson so your score is defensible.
  • You're evaluated on English proficiency, time management, teaching ability, and politeness, so protect all four in every mock.
  • Send the accomplished feedback note in the chat before delivering your verbal feedback.
  • Base every score comment on evidence: the student's exact words, captured during the lesson.

Free Trial Lesson (FTL) mock guide

Straight from RareJob's Ideal FTL Lesson flow. A trial student is nervous, unsure, and deciding whether to subscribe; your job is to make them feel capable and cared for.

  • Before the lesson
    Prepare a notepad with your greeting message, the links of the materials you may use, and your recommendation scripts. Dead air while hunting for links is a classic demo killer.
  • Min 0–2 · Introduction
    Check audio ("Can you hear me clearly? Koe wa kikoemasu ka?"), give a warm brief self-introduction, and find one thread to connect on (anime, travel, food).
  • Min 2–10 · Small talk
    Student-centered conversation with genuine follow-ups. Top FTL student interests: travel, movies, music, shopping, food, sports, cooking, reading. Quietly assess their level and their reason for learning English; it decides your recommendation later.
  • Min 10–23 · Lesson proper
    Send the material link, confirm they can open it. Correct only repeating mistakes, always positively phrased, never interrupting. Vary your praise: "Good job!", "Wonderful!", "That's great!", "Yoku dekimashita!". You don't need to finish the material; you DO need to keep time for the ending.
  • Min 23–24 · Feedback
    Sandwich method with specifics: a concrete strength, one concrete improvement with an example sentence, then encouragement. Ask: "How was the lesson today?"
  • Min 24–25 · Recommendation
    Summarize their need, give assurance ("Don't worry, I will help you!"), then recommend 1–2 materials with the reason and the link in the chat box. Close by warmly encouraging them to continue with RareJob.

Teens & young learners mock guide

RareJob's junior/senior high and teens track (including the Teens Free Trial with its Level Check). The core skill: energy calibrated to a shy teenager, not a business adult.

What changes with young learners

  • Level Check first: the teens FTL includes a level check; keep questions short and concrete ("What subject do you like?" not "Tell me about your academic interests").
  • Shorter turns, more turns: teens fade during long explanations. One instruction at a time, then let them do.
  • Energy up, pressure down: bigger smiles, expressive reactions, visible patience. Count five silently after every question; teen silence is thinking plus shyness.
  • Scaffold, don't rescue: offer sentence starters ("I like... because...") instead of answering for them.
  • Their world: games, anime, club activities (bukatsu), school festivals, favorite YouTubers. One genuine question about their club buys ten minutes of goodwill.
  • Praise effort loudly and specifically: "You made that whole sentence by yourself!" builds the confidence the parents are paying for.

Openers that work with teens

"Hi! I'm Teacher Niza. Don't worry, we will go slowly and mistakes are totally okay here. First, easy question: do you like games or sports?"

"Wow, you're in the basketball club? That's cool! Is practice hard? Tell me one word about your club: fun, hard, or tiring?"

"You did such a great job today! You spoke English for 25 minutes. That's amazing. Next time, let's talk about your favorite game, okay? See you!"

Free Conversation garden

Topics Japanese learners genuinely love, with ready questions, sample answers, and a Japanese lifeline for each question. Tap 🔊 to hear the pronunciation, spoken by your device's Japanese voice.

If it's your first time talking together

  • Start with a short, warm self-introduction: your name, where you're from, and one friendly detail (a hobby or a pet). Then invite theirs: "Could you tell me a little about yourself?"
  • Open with the gentlest topics first: hometown, food, weather, hobbies. Save opinions and abstract questions for when they're warmed up.
  • Ask permission for the vibe: "Would you like me to correct your mistakes, or shall we just enjoy talking today?" Free conversation is always marked Completed in the notes, but correction style is theirs to choose.
  • Topics to lovingly avoid: politics and religion (required by the guidelines), plus salary, age, weight or appearance, and marriage or children status unless the student brings them up first.
  • Let silence breathe. A pause means they're building the sentence. Smile, nod, and wait a few seconds before offering help.
  • Note the gold. Their dog's name, their favorite ramen shop, the trip they're planning: log it in My Students 🐾 so next time you can ask, "How was Kyoto?"
Using the Japanese lifeline: ask in English first, twice, a little slower the second time. If their face says "lost," offer the Japanese version kindly, then guide them back to answering in English. The goal is comfort, not a Japanese lesson.
